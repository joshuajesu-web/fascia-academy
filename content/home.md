## Built to move

For almost all of human history, nobody "worked out." We walked, ran, climbed, carried, threw and wrestled. We moved barefoot over uneven ground, in every direction, at every speed. Training wasn't a session in a calendar. It was life, and it built bodies that were springy, connected and hard to break.

The ancient Greek gymnasium had no machines. Its athletes wrestled, ran, jumped and threw. In China, the *Daoyin* exercises painted on silk more than 2,000 years ago flowed from the ground up through the whole body. Every movement trained the body as **one connected system**.

## Then came the mirror

On 14 September 1901, a Prussian strongman called Eugen Sandow filled London's Royal Albert Hall for "The Great Competition", recognised as the first modern bodybuilding contest. Nobody was judged on how they *moved*. They were judged on how they *looked*.

That one idea, that a body is something to look at, went on to shape the next hundred years of training.

> We started measuring what was easy to measure: inches, kilos and reps. And we stopped measuring what actually matters.

## The age of size

Joe Weider's magazines, from 1940 onwards, sold the physique as the goal. The Mr. Olympia contest arrived in 1965. In 1977, *Pumping Iron* made Arnold Schwarzenegger a household name, and commercial gyms spread across America.

In 1970, Arthur Jones sold his first Nautilus machine. One seat, one strap, one muscle, one fixed path. It was brilliant engineering, and it turned the gym into a row of machines that each train a single muscle in isolation. That model is still the default today.

## What it cost us

A machine moves you along one line. Life never does.

Strap in, isolate, repeat, and the muscle grows. But the connections between the muscles are left untrained: the springy web that links your foot to your glute to your opposite shoulder. Size and aesthetics took priority over function, and our natural movement patterns paid for it.

You see it everywhere. Bodies that look powerful but move stiffly. Big muscles sitting on weak, lazy feet. Strength that doesn't carry over to sprinting, jumping or changing direction. Niggles that become injuries, and injuries that keep coming back.

> I lived this. Years of chronic injuries, physio appointment after physio appointment, and money spent on getting fixed, only to break again. Then I learned to train the connections instead of the parts.
>
> — Josh

## The hidden secret

Under your skin, wrapping every muscle, bone and organ, is one continuous web of connective tissue: **fascia**. For centuries, anatomists cut it away and threw it out to get a better look at the muscles underneath. It wasn't until 2007 that scientists gathered for the first international congress dedicated to it.

Fascia is a big part of why a kangaroo can hop further than its leg muscles alone can explain, why your Achilles tendon stores and returns energy like a spring, and how force travels from the ground through your whole body in one connected line.

Watch the greatest athletes move. Bolt, Messi, Bruce Lee. Relaxed. Elastic. Connected. That is the hidden secret of human athleticism, and it can be trained.
